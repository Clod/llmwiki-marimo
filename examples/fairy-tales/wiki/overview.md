# Knowledge Base Overview

The knowledge base began with a single text: a public-domain French fairy tale, retold by Andrew Lang from Charles Perrault's original. It follows Cinderella, a stepdaughter mistreated in her household, whose life changes when her fairy godmother transforms her so she can attend the King's son's ball. The glass slipper she leaves behind lets the prince find her, and she marries him and forgives her stepsisters, moving the story from persecution to reconciliation. Its core concepts are Cinderella, the fairy godmother, the midnight curfew that creates the tension of her hurried departure, the glass slipper that ties the stranger to her true identity, and the stepmother and stepsisters whose cruelty gives the forgiveness its weight.

The knowledge base now also holds two further tales. A version of *Little Red Riding Hood* from *The Blue Fairy Book* (Andrew Lang, 1889) follows a girl in a red hood who travels through a forest to visit her sick grandmother and meets a wolf who disguises his voice. The wolf eats the grandmother and then the girl, and the story's central concern is deception rather than recognition. Its red riding-hood cloak, like the slipper, is a distinctive marker, but it identifies the heroine rather than revealing a hidden identity. A retelling of *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* adds a third arc. A jealous Queen, consulting her magic mirror, orders the huntsman to kill the fairest princess, who finds refuge with seven dwarfs. A poisoned apple kills her, a prince's kiss revives her, and the wicked Queen dies at the wedding. The new material brings in the magic mirror, the poisoned apple, the Seven Dwarfs, and Snow White herself.

Read together, the tales show recurring patterns. Threats often come from inside or near the family: the stepmother and stepsisters in Cinderella, the wolf who impersonates the grandmother, and the Queen who is the heroine's own stepmother. Disguise and deception drive the plots, whether Cinderella's transformation hides her from the court, the wolf's voice hides him from the girl, or the poisoned apple hides death in something desirable. Objects recur as tokens of identity or danger. The slipper, the cloak, the mirror, and the apple each carry meaning beyond their physical role. Princes appear as the agents of restoration in both Cinderella and Snow White, where the slipper and the kiss each bring the heroine back to the world. The first reading identified a sequence of hardship, supernatural aid, temporary elevation, and restoration. Snow White partly fits this, but its supernatural element works against the heroine: the magic mirror serves the antagonist, and the heroine receives no fairy godmother. Little Red Riding Hood, as summarized, has neither supernatural aid nor restoration and ends in catastrophe. Together these contrasts suggest the Cinderella arc may be a feature of its particular retelling rather than of the folk form as a whole.

The limits of these observations matter. The Cinderella and Little Red Riding Hood texts come from the same anthology and editor, so shared qualities may reflect Lang's editorial choices as much as the wider tradition. The provenance of the Snow White retelling is not recorded in the new summary, so it is unclear whether it shares that editorial influence. Three documents are not enough to establish general patterns. Other versions of these tales, Perrault's originals, and unrelated folklore sharing these concepts would show whether disguise, tokens, and family threat recur across the tradition. They would also show whether the contrast between restorative endings (Cinderella, Snow White) and fatal ones (Little Red Riding Hood) is typical or an exception.
